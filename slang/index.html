02

Anatomy of a word: groovy

One word's whole life, from a jazz musician's word list to your parents' vocabulary, traced through a century of novels.

Groovy started in jazz. To be "in the groove" was to be playing well, probably after a needle sitting snugly in a record's groove. Green's Dictionary of Slang finds groovy in a 1937 list of musicians' words.

For twenty years it barely registers in books.

Then the 1960s happened. In 1970, groovy was more common in novels than at any time before or since.

By 1980 it had become the thing your parents said, and its frequency in novels was down by 77%.

Then it crept back. By 2007 it was 2.7 times as common as at its low, though still only 35% of its peak.

A warning about data. In all English books, not just novels, groovy keeps climbing until 2014. That isn't a second revival: much of it is Groovy, a programming language, which novels rarely mention. That's one reason we count slang in fiction.

03

Where slang comes from

A word's first appearance in print is rarely its first use. These are the communities that dictionaries document as the source of the words in our collection.

African American Vernacular English (AAVE) is a full dialect with its own grammar, not "slang". But many words that crossed from it into general use were later treated as slang, and the credit often went elsewhere, as the story of on fleek below shows.

Dig, jive, far out

Green's Dictionary of Slang labels a long run of mid-century words as originally Black American usage, many of them carried into the mainstream by jazz musicians and their fans.

  • dig (understand) 1936
  • jive 1926
  • square (conformist) 1901
  • daddy-o 1935
  • zoot suit 1942
  • far out 1949
  • hipster 1938

Sources: Green's Dictionary of Slang, dig; Green's Dictionary of Slang, jive

Shade, reading, realness

Ballroom culture, the Black and Latino LGBTQ+ scene in New York, gave mainstream English a whole critical vocabulary. The 1990 documentary Paris Is Burning carried much of it to a wider audience.

  • throw shade 1985
  • read (to insult) 1990
  • realness 1990
  • voguing 1988
  • slay 1990

Sources: Paris Is Burning (Wikipedia); Green's Dictionary of Slang, shade

On fleek

In June 2014, Kayla Newman, a Chicago-area teenager, posted a six-second Vine about her eyebrows. By October, IHOP was tweeting about pancakes on fleek. In 2017 she said she had never been paid or credited for the phrase, and crowdfunded her own beauty line.

Sources: Newsweek; IHOP on X; The FADER, March 2017

04

The cringe pipeline

Slang tends to travel the same road: a subculture coins it, teenagers adopt it, everyone hears it, brands and parents use it, and it's over.

  1. 1Coined
  2. 2Goes viral
  3. 3Everyone's looking it up
  4. 4Brands and dictionaries
  5. 5Cringe

The tell-tale sign of the middle stage is a surge of people looking a word up. When the people searching for a word are the people who don't know it, it has left home.

05

Is slang dying faster?

It feels as if words now burn out in months. We tried to measure it.

For words in books, we measured how long each stayed above a quarter of its peak frequency once it had peaked.

Words that took off between 1970 and 1999 don't seem to die any faster than words that took off before 1940. If anything, the reverse: half the older words had faded within 34 years, while only 1 of the 22 newer ones has faded at all.

But two things bias this. Fiction got much chattier after 1970: "yeah" is 5.2 times as common in novels now as around 1950, and "okay" 8.3 times. That props up every colloquial word.

So we divided every curve by how chatty novels were each year. Every group now fades sooner, but the order holds: half the pre-1940 words faded within 14 years, the 1970 to 1999 words within 36.

And the oldest slang we can measure is the slang that survived long enough to be written down. Short-lived slang from the 1920s mostly left no trace in print.

Online, some words really do burn out in months: searches for OK boomer fell below a quarter of their peak 7 months after peaking. But half the internet-era words we track stayed above that line for 8 years or more. And searches measure confusion, not use: once everyone knows rizz, nobody needs to look it up.

So: maybe. The data we have can't tell a faster slang cycle apart from a different way of measuring it.

Methods

How we did this

  • 267 words, each with a dated, sourced first record
  • 135 with a curve from books
  • 47 with a curve from searches or lookups
  • 85 with no reliable curve

First appearances come from Green's Dictionary of Slang, cross-checked against Merriam-Webster, or, for a few words, a documented first use. We record the year of the earliest citation of the specific slang sense, and link to it.

Frequency curves for older words come from the Google Books Ngram Viewer's English Fiction corpus (2024 dataset, to 2022), smoothed over five years. Books lag speech, skew formal and Western, and get chattier over time.

For internet-era words we use Google Trends (search interest, captured by hand) and Wiktionary pageviews (lookups). Both measure people looking a word up, not using it.

Many slang words share their spelling with an ordinary word ("lit", "salty", "far out"). We don't show curves for those: counting them would measure the ordinary word.

Generations are marketing labels with fuzzy edges. We assign each word to the generation aged 15 to 24 when it went mainstream.

The full method, its limitations and every source are on the methodology page.
