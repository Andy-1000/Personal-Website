Methodology

How the data behind Bussin' to Groovy were made, and what they can and can't tell you. Numbers are regenerated by scripts/12_summary.py into data/processed/summary.md.

1. Choosing the words

We started from about 300 candidate terms from the 1900s to 2025, chosen to span every generation, Britain and Australia as well as the US, and a range of source communities. Candidates came from our own knowledge and from Wikipedia's lists of slang by decade, used only as an index: no fact in the dataset comes from those lists.

A candidate stays in the dataset only if it has a dated, sourced first attestation (section 2). The ones that don't are listed as gaps in the summary. Recent slang suffers most here, because dictionaries are slow to catch up.

2. First attestation: when a word was first written down

  • Green's Dictionary of Slang (GDoS, Jonathon Green, free online since
    1. is the primary source. For each term we chose the specific slang sense that matches our definition (data/raw/sense_picks.csv) and took the year of its earliest dated citation. Choosing the sense matters: "cool" meaning "calm" goes back to the 1700s, but "cool" meaning "good" dates from
  • Merriam-Webster's "First Known Use" is the cross-check, and the source when GDoS has no matching sense or its first citation is clearly too late (for example, GDoS's earliest "bruh" is from 2023). Merriam-Webster gives one date per entry, for its primary sense, so it's only used for words whose entry is the slang sense.
  • For a handful of words neither dictionary dates in the right sense, we use a documented first use with its source: the 1990 documentary Paris Is Burning for the ballroom words "read", "realness" and "slay"; a 1917 letter to Winston Churchill for "OMG"; a 1989 online newsletter for "LOL"; and Know Your Meme's timelines for "OK boomer" and "fanum tax" (data/raw/manual_attestations.csv).
  • Where no source dates the slang sense, the word is dropped rather than dated by its ordinary sense (marked DROP in sense_picks.csv): Merriam-Webster's 1776 "yas" is a spelling of "yes", and its 1977 "NPC" is the video-game character, not the insult.

A first attestation is the earliest use a lexicographer has found. Spoken slang is almost always older than its first appearance in print, and dictionaries keep finding earlier examples (GDoS has added around 10,000 antedatings since 2010). Treat every date as "by at least".

We store only dates, sense numbers, short usage labels and links. No definitions or quotations are copied from either dictionary; definitions and example sentences in the dataset are our own.

3. Frequency curves: how common a word was

Books (Google Books Ngram Viewer, 2024 dataset, to 2022)

  • We use the English Fiction corpus, not all English books. Dialogue in novels is where slang reaches print, and fiction avoids some technical homonyms: in all English books "groovy" keeps climbing until 2014 (5-year average), much of it Groovy, a programming language, while in novels it peaks in 1970.
  • Values are occurrences per million words, each year.
  • Spike filter. Single-year spikes from one unusual book or a scanning error are replaced by the local median (a Hampel filter: 7-year window, 3 scaled median absolute deviations, upward outliers only). Many modern words show a blip around 1900: books misdated to 1899/1900 are a known Google Books metadata problem.
  • Smoothing. A 5-year centred mean. (We planned 3 years; the fiction sub-corpus is small enough that 3 left too much noise.)
  • Share of peak. Each curve is divided by its own maximum, so shapes can be compared between words of very different frequency.
  • Quality review. Every curve was inspected by eye (data/raw/curve_review.csv) and rated clean, low (too rare to be more than noisy) or contaminated (another sense or a proper noun dominates: "far out" at sea, the sausage called baloney, Yolo County). Contaminated curves are never shown. Some words use a documented stand-in phrase: "really cool" for cool, "you guys" for guys, "okay" for OK, the apostrophe-free spelling for possessives such as "bees knees" (the Ngram query parser can't handle apostrophes).

Online (Google Trends and Wiktionary)

  • Google Trends has no public API (the official one is an application-gated alpha, and the community library pytrends was archived in April 2025). We captured monthly worldwide search interest, January 2004 to September 2026, one term at a time, by hand in a browser. We searched the bare word when it is distinctive ("rizz") and "⟨word⟩ meaning" when the bare word has a common ordinary sense ("slay meaning"). Each series is on its own 0–100 scale, so only shapes are comparable. Single-month surges (one news story or viral post) are damped with the same spike filter used for books, and curves are smoothed over 3 months. The capture (5 October 2026) is stored run-length encoded with a length, sum and position-weighted sum computed in the browser; the import step checks all three, so a copying error fails the build instead of entering the data.
  • Wiktionary pageviews (Wikimedia API, from July 2015) count people looking a word up. Only pages that are distinctively slang are used: the page for "lit" or "salty" mostly measures the ordinary word. Single-month bot spikes are filtered, and curves smoothed over 3 months.
  • Both measure looking a word up, not using it. A word stops being looked up once everyone knows it. That makes online curves good evidence for when a word reached outsiders, and poor evidence for how long it was used.

Sources are never stitched together: they measure different things on different scales.

4. Lifecycle measures

For each curve (scaled so the peak is 1):

  • Peak: the year (or month) of the maximum.
  • Popularised: the first time the curve reaches 10% of its peak (25% for the monthly online series, whose noise floor for low-volume searches often sits near 10%), no earlier than the first attestation of the chosen sense. Only curves rated clean are used; low-count curves are too noisy to date anything, and a curve already above the threshold in the first year of its source's data can't date a word either (Wiktionary starts in July 2015, after "cray" took off). Where Wiktionary lookups do date a word, the date marks when outsiders started looking it up, which can trail its use. Words without a usable curve get a best guess, made by whichever method best predicted the year for the 139 words whose clean curve gives the answer (each word left out of its own test):
    • Green's citations (102 words): the year by which a fifth of Green's dated citations of the chosen sense had appeared, where there are three or more. Unbiased, median error 5 years, within 5 years for 53% of the test words.
    • Typical lag (the other 23): first attestation plus the median lag seen in clean-curve words first recorded in the same era: about 72 years before 1900, 13 for 1900–39, 6 for 1940–69, 7.5 for 1970–89 and 5 from
      1. Median error 7.5 years, within 5 years for 31%. The story shows these years like any other; they are marked in the data (popularised_basis: citations or lag). Three words whose only usable curve misdates them have a documented year instead (data/raw/popularised_overrides.csv, each with its source): "OK" (1840, the Van Buren campaign), "cool" (1947, cool jazz) and "dude" (1990, its Teenage Mutant Ninja Turtles revival).
  • Fall: the first time after the peak that the curve drops below 25% of peak and stays there for 3 years (6 months for monthly data).
  • Revived: after a fall, the curve climbs back above 50% of peak.
  • Status: revived, dated (fell, no revival), alive (never fell), ironic (only where a cited source says so) or unknown (no curve).

5. Generations

A word belongs to the generation aged about 15–24 in the year it was popularised (birth year ≈ popularised year − 19.5). Boundaries: Lost 1883–1900 and Greatest 1901–1927 (Strauss & Howe); Silent 1928–45, Boomers 1946–64, Gen X 1965–80, Millennials 1981–96, Gen Z 1997–2012 (Pew); Gen Alpha 2013–24. Generation labels are marketing constructs with fuzzy edges, and slang is used across ages; treat the colours as a rough guide.

Under this rule no word can belong to Gen Alpha yet (its oldest members are 13). Terms that cited sources describe as Gen Alpha slang and that were first attested in 2022 or later are labelled Gen Alpha as a stated exception (data/raw/gen_alpha_sources.csv).

6. Origins

Each term's source community comes first from GDoS's usage labels and etymologies ("orig. US black", "orig. US milit.", "US campus", "orig. surfing"). For terms GDoS gives no community, we checked Wiktionary's etymologies (which cite their sources), Wikipedia and Merriam-Webster, and assigned a community only where one of them names it: "stan" from Eminem's 2000 song, "beatnik" from the columnist Herb Caen, "bruh" from African American English. Each assignment links to its source (data/raw/origins.csv). Most remaining words have only a word-formation etymology ("short for suspicious"), which says nothing about who first used them; they are shown as having no recorded source community. Each has a confidence level:

  • documented: the source says so explicitly;
  • likely: the source suggests it, with hedging ("probably", "supposedly");
  • unknown: no source says, or etymologies are disputed.

We report what the sources document and no more. African American Vernacular English (AAVE) is a dialect with its own grammar, not slang; GDoS labels many words as originally Black American usage, and we describe them as words that crossed from AAVE into general use.

7. Is slang dying faster?

Each word with a usable curve is drawn as a line from the year it took off (the same year the rest of the story uses) to the year it faded: fell below a quarter of its peak and stayed there for 3 years (6 months for searches). Words that haven't faded are censored, not dropped. Groups are compared by the share that faded within a fixed span after taking off (20 years for books, which every group has been followed for; 5 years online), estimated with Kaplan–Meier. Books and searches are never put on the same scale.

An earlier version measured years above a quarter of the peak after the peak; those survival figures are still computed (survival.json) and point the same way.

Known biases, all of which we report rather than correct:

  • Fiction got chattier. Informal words such as "yeah", "okay" and "gonna" are several times more frequent in novels since the 1970s. That props up every colloquial word's recent frequency and delays its fall. We rerun the analysis after dividing each curve by an informality index (the mean of those three words, each scaled to its 1950–2022 average); both versions are published.
  • Survivorship. Old slang that died quickly mostly never reached print or dictionaries, so the oldest cohorts are made of hardy words.
  • Right-censoring. Recent words have had little time to fall.
  • Different instruments. Online lookups fall faster than usage does, so online lifespans are not comparable with book lifespans and are shown separately.

8. Other limitations

  • Book corpora skew formal, edited, Western and American.
  • First attestations depend on what lexicographers have searched; some eras and communities are better searched than others.
  • Polysemous words (lit, bad, sick, slay) have no clean frequency curve.
  • Brand adoption is shown only where it is documented with a source; it is an illustration, not a measurement.
  • The Google Trends capture is a snapshot (retrieved 5 October 2026); the same queries may return slightly different values later.

9. Reproducing

python scripts/run_all.py --offline rebuilds everything from the committed raw data. Without --offline it refetches whatever is not in the local cache (data/raw/_cache/, not committed).
