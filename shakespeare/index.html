The famous number

Where “1,700 words” comes from

~1,700 words Shakespeare is said to have invented

The claim usually goes like this: Shakespeare invented about 1,700 words.

The number has a real source. The linguist David Crystal searched the second edition of the Oxford English Dictionary for every word whose earliest quotation came from Shakespeare. He found 2,079.

He set aside malapropisms and nonsense words, the kind Shakespeare gives his clowns, which left 2,035.

Then he estimated how many Shakespeare had really coined: the words nobody else used for 25 years, plus half of those other writers picked up quickly. That’s where 1,700 comes from.

Notice what it counts: quotations in a dictionary, not inventions. So how would anyone know who invented a word?

How he made them

Mostly from parts English already had

Most of these words weren’t conjured from nothing. They were built from parts English already had.

The biggest group adds an ending to a word: -less, -ful, -ment, -ness, -able.

Others add a beginning: un-, dis-, be-, out-, over-. Plenty do both.

Some glue two words together.

And some put an old word to a new job: a noun used as a verb, or a familiar word given a new sense.

The plot twist

Then the dictionary started reading everyone else

The famous number comes from a dictionary that read Shakespeare far more closely than the people around him. Since 2000 it has been catching up.

In 1989 the second edition of the Oxford English Dictionary quoted Shakespeare as the first user of 2,035 words, by David Crystal’s count. Its entries then stood, unrevised, until 2000.

In 2000 the OED began its first full revision, with something its first editors never had: thousands of early books, transcribed and searchable. By June 2019 Shakespeare’s tally had fallen to 1,467.

The revision isn’t finished. In the stretch of the alphabet it had reached by 2010, M to rotness, about a third of his firsts were gone. Charlotte Brewer, who studies the OED’s history, projects about 1,200 by the end.

We took 400 entries from Crystal’s list, checked each against the OED as it stands today, and searched 60,000 early printed books for older uses. 141 are still first recorded in Shakespeare. 62 have an earlier use, and most of the rest are disputed or not yet settled.

Dozens are disputed rather than lost. The OED dates a quotation by the edition it’s printed in. Thomas Nashe printed swagger in 1599; A Midsummer Night’s Dream was written years earlier but not printed until 1600, so the dictionary gives the word to Nashe.

The earlier uses aren’t near misses. The typical gap is about 30 years, and some words were in use more than a century before Shakespeare was born.

Even phrases people quote as his were in print first. Truth will out appears in a 1559 poem about the Duke of Clarence, and Thomas Hoby wrote of infinite variety in his 1561 translation of Castiglione.

Laughing stock is in an English prayer book from 1534, and the OED now traces it to around 1518. Eaten out of house and home turns up in a 1578 tract against moneylenders.

Our search also turned up uses the dictionary doesn’t record. The OED’s earliest invitation is Shakespeare’s, in 1602; we found it in a book printed around 1563, and John Knox wrote impeded in 1556. We count these as possible, not proven, until they’re checked against the OED’s full entries.

Special, or just well read?

Was he special, or just read more closely?

The OED quotes Shakespeare about 33,000 times, more than twice as often as any other writer. More quotations mean more chances to be first. So we also counted another way: one rule, applied to five writers of his time, in the same 60,000 books.

On the dictionary’s count, nobody comes close to Shakespeare. On ours, he doesn’t stand out. Thomas Nashe, a pamphleteer famous for his wild vocabulary, prints far more new words that other writers went on to use.

Dating Shakespeare’s plays by when they were written rather than when they were printed roughly doubles his rate, close to Spenser’s. The ranges are wide and they overlap, so the fairest reading is that he was one inventive writer among several, and the one the dictionary read most closely.

How we did this

Methods and sources

The list. David Crystal’s tables of every OED entry where Shakespeare was the first recorded user, as of July 2017: 2,531 entries, including 373 that had already been antedated. From these we drew 400 to check in depth: every entry that appears on a popular “Shakespeare invented” list, a random sample spread across common, rare and obsolete words, and 84 well-known phrases.

The dictionary today. For each of the 400 we read the OED’s public factsheet in October 2026: its current earliest evidence and whether the entry has been revised. The factsheet covers the whole entry, not each sense, so when Shakespeare is credited with a new sense of an older word, an earlier date elsewhere in the entry may be about another sense. We mark those as disputed.

Earlier uses. We searched the 60,326 books in EEBO-TCP, transcriptions of English books printed up to 1700, for every word and phrase, allowing for old spellings (vnbosomd, inuitation). A match counts only if a person has read it in context and it’s the same word in the same sense, printed before Shakespeare began the play. Most matches are other words or other senses. Dates and ESTC numbers come from each book’s catalogue record; a date in brackets is the cataloguer’s inference. Where we found a use earlier than the OED’s own evidence, we call it possible until it can be checked against the full entry.

Dates. Play dates are ranges from Crystal’s Shakespeare’s Words. The OED dates Shakespeare by the edition it quotes, so a later edition can lose to another writer printed in between. We call those disputed, not antedated.

Frequency. 1550–1700: matches per million words in EEBO-TCP, by decade, not reviewed by hand. 1820–2022: Google Books Ngram (English, 2024 data), smoothed. “Common today” uses Robyn Speer’s wordfreq.

The comparison. For Shakespeare, Marlowe, Jonson, Nashe and Spenser, we counted words that first appear in EEBO-TCP in one of the writer’s own books and were later used by at least two other books. A person sorted samples of 40 per writer into genuine words, misprints, foreign words and names, and each rate is scaled by its share of genuine words. The ranges allow for both how much the rate moves between random samples of each writer’s text and the uncertainty in that share.

Limits. EEBO-TCP is print only and covers a selection of books; speech and manuscripts leave no trace in it. Our sample over-represents famous words. Quotations from Shakespeare follow the Folger edition, which modernises spelling.

What’s still open

  • The OED’s full entries (beyond the public factsheets) haven’t been checked, so claims about senses, and the eight possible earlier uses we found, are marked as unconfirmed.
  • Phrases need the full entries too: 71 of our 84 phrases are marked “not yet settled”.
  • Some plays were written with other playwrights. Where a word comes from a collaborative play, we haven’t checked which writer wrote that scene.
