    How this was made

    Names come from the Social Security Administration’s national baby names data, built from applications for Social Security cards, for every year from 1880 to 2024. To protect privacy, a name only appears in a year if at least five babies of one sex got it, so rare names are undercounted. Popularity means a name’s share of all the listed babies of its sex born that year. The records are thin before about 1915, because fewer people born then ever applied for a card, which is why the floor of the chart drops around then.

    A name’s peak is the year its share was highest, and its half-life is the number of years from that peak until its share first fell below half. A name counts as dying, fading, steady or rising by comparing its average share in the last three years with the same three years a decade earlier.

    The number still alive is an estimate. Each birth year is weighted by the chance that someone born then is alive in 2026, using the SSA’s cohort life tables (Actuarial Study 120, interpolated between decades). People born before 1937 only appear because they lived long enough to apply for a card, so their odds start from that point. Migration isn’t counted. The approach follows FiveThirtyEight’s How to Tell Someone’s Age When All You Know Is Her Name.
